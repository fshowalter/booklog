Stephen King's first editor was John Gould of the _Lisbon Weekly Enterprise_. Gould hired the teenage King to cover high school sports, then went through his first article with a black pen. King says he learned more from that edit than from any English class. Gould told him that writing is telling yourself the story, and rewriting is taking out everything that isn't the story.

_On Writing_, published in 2000, spends its first hundred pages as a memoir, and King tells it as if he were talking to you at the kitchen table. As a small boy he believed details were "dentals." He also thought a bitch was a very tall woman, which made a son of a bitch a likely basketball player. At six, he explains, "most of your Bingo balls are still floating around in the draw-tank." Some of mine are still floating. I have the reviews to prove it.

The memoir moves fast. Tabitha King fishes the first pages of <span data-title-id="carrie-by-stephen-king">_Carrie_</span> out of the wastebasket and tells him to finish it, the paperback sale makes them rich, and the beer and cocaine arrive on schedule. He says he barely remembers writing <span data-title-id="cujo-by-stephen-king">_Cujo_</span>. I have reviewed movies whose directors could say the same about shooting them. Tabitha forces the issue by gathering up the trash from his office, coke spoons included, and dumping it on the floor in front of the family. The scene could veer into melodrama, but King gives it a few plain pages and gets back to work.

The second part is the manual, and I don't know a better one. He tells you to read a lot and write a lot. Write the first draft with the door closed, he says, and the second with it open. He warns that "the road to hell is paved with adverbs." Years ago a rejection slip gave him a formula he still uses: 2nd Draft = 1st Draft - 10%. Near the end of the book he shows it at work, printing the opening of his story "1408" twice, once as he first wrote it and once with his cuts marked and explained. I have applied the formula to this review, and you should have seen the first draft. 

Where I part company with King is plot, which he distrusts. He starts with a situation, drops some characters into it and watches what they do. He calls plotting the good writer's last resort and the dullard's first choice. Working from situations gives his novels their long, absorbing middles, and I suspect it accounts for some of the endings. In the movies, stories built this way tend to end with a helicopter.

He allows one exception. <span data-title-id="the-dead-zone-by-stephen-king">_The Dead Zone_</span> is the only novel of his built on plot that he admits to liking, and he likes it a lot. So do I, and I wish he met plot halfway more often. He confesses that he nearly abandoned <span data-title-id="the-stand-by-stephen-king">_The Stand_</span> when he got stuck five hundred pages in. He got it moving again by blowing up some of his main characters, and the book's climax comes when the hand of God reaches down and sets off an atomic bomb. Critics use the term _deus ex machina_ loosely. Here it is a plain description.

The postscript begins on June 19, 1999. King was walking along a road near his summer house in western Maine when a van driven by Bryan Smith hit him. Smith had been distracted by his Rottweiler, Bullet, who was trying to get into a cooler in the back. King's right leg and hip were shattered and a lung collapsed. He went back to his desk about five weeks later, unable to sit for long and in pain the whole time. 

Later, King learned why Smith was on the road at all. Smith had left his campground, he told an investigator, because he wanted "some of those Marzes-bars they have up to the store." King's thought on hearing it: "I have nearly been killed by a character right out of one of my own novels." The sentence comes from a man who spent pages warning writers away from adverbs, and it contains one. He was right to keep it. Take out "nearly" and you have an obituary.
