After _Evil Dead II_ opened, and before it had paid him anything, Bruce Campbell worked the midnight shift as a security guard at the Anheuser-Busch plant. A coworker recognized him from the movies. I assume it was the chin. Campbell tells the story without a trace of grievance, and he tells the rest of his memoir the same way. He seems to regard a paycheck as a happy ending.

He grew up the youngest of three brothers in suburban Detroit. In high school he fell in with Sam Raimi and Scott Spiegel, who were making 8mm movies on pooled equipment. College didn't take. The friends decided to try a real picture, on 16mm with real actors, and they chose horror because one scene in one of their home movies had made an audience scream. They raised money from family, friends and local businessmen, and the Campbells mortgaged their lake property. The family kept the lake. The investors waited six years to be repaid, and Campbell reports this with the candor of a man who has since learned how distributors count.

_The Evil Dead_ shot in a Tennessee cabin during a bitter winter and ran long because Raimi kept inventing new places to put the camera. The best-known result is the shot that tears through the woods, made by bolting the camera to a plank and running. Campbell spent weeks cold and sticky with stage blood. They wrapped with most of a movie and had to raise more money to finish it. In the New York editing room Raimi met an assistant editor named Joel Coen, who, with his brother Ethan, would write _Crimewave_ with Raimi before _Blood Simple_ made them famous.

Campbell was expected to star in _Crimewave_ until the studio insisted on a name. He had a name, but nobody there had heard it, so he got the heel part. No actor could have saved that picture, but it started a pattern: the people writing checks kept seeing a supporting player. Campbell is a star in the old sense, like John Wayne or Clint Eastwood, at his best playing a version of himself the audience has already agreed to like. That version is a braggart who takes a terrible beating and gets up still bragging. Raimi understood this, and the two of them made _Evil Dead II_, half sequel and half remake, which proved the first one was no fluke.

This made Campbell a name, but only in B movies. On the way to the set of _Sundown: The Vampire in Retreat_, a vampire Western, the van driver gushed to David Carradine, "I still have my _Kung Fu_ lunch box." Carradine admitted that he did too. He had carried his lunch in it during the second season. On _Army of Darkness_, Campbell fought skeletons that would be added later through a process called Introvision, ducking and slashing on a count while Raimi shouted the numbers through a megaphone. On _The Adventures of Brisco County, Jr._, the craft service man also cleaned up after the horses, a union arrangement Campbell calls "beyond my comprehension." 

His one serious try for the A-list was an audition for _The Phantom_. Billy Zane got the part, and Zane was right for it. A hero in a purple bodysuit has to believe in the suit, and Campbell always looks like a man about to laugh at his own costume.

_Brisco_ lasted one season on Fox. It was the closest anyone came to building a show around him. If somebody with Jason Blum's habits had been in business in 1982, handing out small budgets and leaving filmmakers alone, Raimi and Campbell might have made a picture every other year. 

He marries twice in the course of the book and covers both marriages with the economy of a man who knows his ex-wife will read it. The story ends as he finishes his years as a guest star and occasional director on _Hercules: The Legendary Journeys_.

I listened to the audio edition, which Campbell reads himself, and it's like hearing him tell the whole thing across a table in a bar. I would order another round to keep him talking, and I suspect he would let me pay.
